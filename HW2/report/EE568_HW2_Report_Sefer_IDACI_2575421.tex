\subsection{Step 1 -- Derived Quantities}

\subsubsection{Electrical Angle per Slot $\alpha_e$}

\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\alpha_e = \frac{p \times 360\degree}{N_s}}
  = \frac{3 \times 360\degree}{72} = \mathbf{15\degree\,\text{(electrical)}}
  \label{eq:alpha_e}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Slots per Pole per Phase $q$}

The total number of slots is shared equally among $2p$ poles and $m$ phases:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{q = \frac{N_s}{2p \cdot m}}
  = \frac{72}{6 \times 3} = \mathbf{4}
  \label{eq:q}
\end{equation}
Because $q = 4 \in \mathbb{Z}^+$, this is an \emph{integral-slot} winding.
Each phase occupies 4 consecutive slots under each pole.

\subsubsection{Pole Pitch $\tau_p$}

The pole pitch is the number of slots under one pole:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\tau_p = \frac{N_s}{2p}}
  = \frac{72}{6} = \mathbf{12\,\text{slots}}
  \label{eq:tau_p}
\end{equation}
A \emph{full-pitched} coil spans exactly $\tau_p = 12$ slots.

\subsubsection{One Pole-Pair $= 360\degree$ electrical $= 24$ slots}

Since $\alpha_e = 15\degree$, it takes $360\degree / 15\degree = 24$ slots
to complete one full electrical cycle.
This means \textbf{one pole-pair spans 24 consecutive slots}, which is the
minimum repeating unit of the winding.

% ---- 1.5 -------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Step 4 -- Distribution Factor $k_d$}

\subsubsection{Physical Meaning}

In a concentrated winding (all $q$ coils of one phase under one pole placed
in a single slot) the coil EMFs are perfectly in phase and add arithmetically.
In a \emph{distributed} winding the $q$ coils are spread over $q$ adjacent
slots, each displaced by $\alpha_e$ electrically.
Their EMF phasors form an arc of a regular polygon, and the vector sum is
smaller than the arithmetic sum.

\begin{equation}
  k_d = \frac{\text{vector (phasor) sum of the }q\text{ coil EMFs}}
             {\text{arithmetic sum of the }q\text{ coil EMFs}}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Derivation}

Each of the $q$ phasors has magnitude $E_m$ and successive phasors are
offset by $\alpha_e$.
The vector sum of $q$ unit phasors starting at $0\degree$ and stepping by $\alpha_e$:
\begin{equation}
  \left|\sum_{i=0}^{q-1} e^{j\,i\,\alpha_e}\right|
  = \frac{\sin(q\,\alpha_e/2)}{\sin(\alpha_e/2)}
\end{equation}
(standard result for a finite geometric series on the unit circle).
The arithmetic sum is simply $q$.
Hence:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{k_{d,n} = \frac{\sin\!\left(\dfrac{n\,q\,\alpha_e}{2}\right)}
                        {q\,\sin\!\left(\dfrac{n\,\alpha_e}{2}\right)}}
  \label{eq:kd_q1}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the harmonic order.
For the $n$-th harmonic the electrical angle between adjacent phasors becomes
$n\,\alpha_e$, so $\alpha_e$ is replaced by $n\,\alpha_e$ throughout.

\subsubsection{Calculations for $q=4$, $\alpha_e = 15\degree$}

\textbf{Fundamental ($n=1$):}
\begin{align}
  k_{d,1} &= \frac{\sin\!\left(\dfrac{1 \times 4 \times 15\degree}{2}\right)}
                  {4\,\sin\!\left(\dfrac{1 \times 15\degree}{2}\right)}
           = \frac{\sin 30\degree}{4\,\sin 7.5\degree}
           = \frac{0.5000}{4 \times 0.1305}
           = \frac{0.5000}{0.5221}
           = \mathbf{0.9577}
\end{align}

\textbf{Third harmonic ($n=3$):}
\begin{align}
  k_{d,3} &= \frac{\sin\!\left(\dfrac{3 \times 4 \times 15\degree}{2}\right)}
                  {4\,\sin\!\left(\dfrac{3 \times 15\degree}{2}\right)}
           = \frac{\sin 90\degree}{4\,\sin 22.5\degree}
           = \frac{1.0000}{4 \times 0.3827}
           = \frac{1.0000}{1.5307}
           = \mathbf{0.6533}
\end{align}

\textbf{Fifth harmonic ($n=5$):}
\begin{align}
  k_{d,5} &= \frac{\sin\!\left(\dfrac{5 \times 4 \times 15\degree}{2}\right)}
                  {4\,\sin\!\left(\dfrac{5 \times 15\degree}{2}\right)}
           = \frac{\sin 150\degree}{4\,\sin 37.5\degree}
           = \frac{0.5000}{4 \times 0.6088}
           = \frac{0.5000}{2.4351}
           = \mathbf{0.2053}
\end{align}

\noindent The distribution factor \emph{always decreases with harmonic order}
for a fixed $q$ -- distributing the winding inherently attenuates higher harmonics.

% ---- 1.6 -------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Step 5 -- Pitch Factor $k_p$}

\subsubsection{Physical Meaning}

Consider one coil with its go side in slot $k$ and its return side in
slot $k+y$.
The EMF induced in the go-side conductor is
$e_\text{go}(t) = E_m \sin(\omega t)$.
The EMF induced in the return-side conductor is
$e_\text{ret}(t) = E_m \sin(\omega t - \theta_c)$
where $\theta_c = y\,\alpha_e$ is the electrical angle spanned by the coil.
Because the return conductor is wound backwards (it is the return path of the
coil), its contribution to the \emph{net} coil EMF is $-e_\text{ret}$.
The total coil EMF is:
\begin{equation}
  e_\text{coil} = e_\text{go} - e_\text{ret}
                = E_m\bigl[\sin\omega t - \sin(\omega t - \theta_c)\bigr]
                = 2E_m\cos\!\left(\omega t - \frac{\theta_c}{2}\right)
                  \sin\!\frac{\theta_c}{2}
\end{equation}
The amplitude is $2E_m\sin(\theta_c/2)$.
For a \emph{full-pitched} coil $\theta_c = 180\degree$, so the amplitude is
$2E_m\sin(90\degree)=2E_m$ (maximum possible).
The \emph{pitch factor} is the ratio of the short-pitch amplitude to the
full-pitch amplitude:
\begin{equation}
  k_p = \frac{2E_m\sin(\theta_c/2)}{2E_m\sin(90\degree)}
      = \sin\!\frac{\theta_c}{2}
      = \sin\!\frac{y\,\alpha_e}{2}
      = \sin\!\frac{y\,\pi}{2\tau_p}
\end{equation}
For the $n$-th harmonic the field rotates $n$ times faster, so the coil
spans $n$ times more electrical angle:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{k_{p,n} = \sin\!\left(\frac{n\,y\,\pi}{2\,\tau_p}\right)}
  \label{eq:kp_q1}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Calculations}

\textbf{Full-pitched ($y = 12$, $\tau_p = 12$):}
\begin{align}
  k_{p,1} &= \sin\!\frac{1 \times 12\,\pi}{2 \times 12} = \sin\frac{\pi}{2} = \mathbf{1.0000}\\
  k_{p,3} &= \left|\sin\!\frac{3 \times 12\,\pi}{2 \times 12}\right|
           = \left|\sin\frac{3\pi}{2}\right| = |-1| = \mathbf{1.0000}\\
  k_{p,5} &= \left|\sin\!\frac{5 \times 12\,\pi}{2 \times 12}\right|
           = \left|\sin\frac{5\pi}{2}\right| = |+1| = \mathbf{1.0000}
\end{align}
All harmonics have $k_p = 1$ -- a full-pitched coil gives maximum EMF for every harmonic.

\textbf{11/12 Short-pitched ($y = 11$, $\tau_p = 12$):}
\begin{align}
  k_{p,1} &= \sin\!\frac{1 \times 11\,\pi}{2 \times 12}
           = \sin\!\frac{11\pi}{24}
           = \sin 82.5\degree = \mathbf{0.9914}\\
  k_{p,3} &= \left|\sin\!\frac{3 \times 11\,\pi}{2 \times 12}\right|
           = \left|\sin\!\frac{33\pi}{24}\right|
           = \left|\sin 247.5\degree\right|
           = |-\sin 67.5\degree| = \mathbf{0.9239}\\
  k_{p,5} &= \left|\sin\!\frac{5 \times 11\,\pi}{2 \times 12}\right|
           = \left|\sin\!\frac{55\pi}{24}\right|
           = \left|\sin 412.5\degree\right|
           = \sin 52.5\degree = \mathbf{0.7934}
\end{align}

\subsection{Discussion and Comments}

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Distribution factor decreases with harmonic order.}
  For $q=4$ and $\alpha_e=15\degree$, going from $n=1$ to $n=5$ drops
  $k_d$ from 0.958 to only 0.205.
  Spreading the winding over $q$ slots is therefore a powerful tool for
  harmonic attenuation, even before any pitch adjustment.

\item \textbf{Full-pitched winding: pitch factor is unity for all odd harmonics.}
  Because $y\alpha_e = 180\degree$, the formula $k_{p,n}=\sin(n\pi/2)$
  gives $\pm 1$ for every odd $n$.
  This means the full-pitched winding provides \emph{no} pitch-based harmonic
  suppression -- the 3rd harmonic winding factor of 0.653 is entirely due to
  $k_d$ and remains high.

\item \textbf{Short-pitching disproportionately suppresses higher harmonics.}
  Shortening by just 1 slot ($y = 11$) changes $k_{p,1}$ by only $-0.9\%$
  but reduces $k_{p,5}$ by $-20.6\%$.
  The mathematical reason: $k_{p,n}=\sin(n\cdot y\pi/2\tau_p)$ is more sensitive
  to changes in $y$ for larger $n$ because the argument is multiplied by $n$.
  A small shift in $y$ sweeps a larger angle for the 5th harmonic than for the
  fundamental.

\item \textbf{The 3rd harmonic is a triplen harmonic.}
  In a balanced 3-phase machine, triplen ($n = 3, 9, 15,\ldots$) space
  harmonics do not produce average torque because they are in phase across
  all three phases (no 120\degree\ offset between phases for $n=3$).
  However, they still cause iron losses and noise.


\item \textbf{Summary recommendation:}
  The 11/12 short-pitched winding is the better design.
  It costs $<1\%$ of useful torque capability while achieving significant
  attenuation of harmful harmonics.
  In industrial practice, short pitching is almost universally applied
  in distributed-winding AC machines.

\end{enumerate}

\noindent The slots per pole per phase is:
\begin{equation}
  q = \frac{N_s}{N_m \cdot m} = \frac{15}{8 \times 3} = 0.625 \notin \mathbb{Z}^+
\end{equation}
Because $q$ is not an integer, the simple coil-group rule of integral-slot
windings (``$q$ consecutive slots per phase per pole'') does not apply,
and the closed-form formulas for $k_d$ and $k_p$ are invalid.
Instead, the \emph{star of slots} phasor method must be used.

% ---- 2.1 -------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Step 1 -- Electrical Angle per Slot $\alpha_s$}

For a machine with $N_m$ poles the field repeats every pole pair
($360\degree$ electrical).
The electrical angle between two adjacent slots is:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\alpha_s = \frac{N_m \times 180\degree}{N_s}}
  = \frac{8 \times 180\degree}{15} = \mathbf{96\degree}
  \label{eq:alpha_s}
\end{equation}
Note that $\alpha_s = 96\degree > 60\degree$: each step jumps \emph{across}
a sector boundary rather than staying within one 60\degree\ phase group.
This is the signature of a fractional-slot winding -- the slot phasors do not
cluster neatly into phase groups.

% ---- 2.2 -------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Step 2 -- Slot Phasor Angles (Star of Slots)}

For a fractional-slot winding the winding factor cannot be obtained from
simple closed-form expressions; the \emph{star of slots} (phasor diagram)
method is used instead.
Each slot $k$ is assigned an EMF phasor whose electrical angle is:
\begin{equation}
  \varphi_k = (k-1)\,\alpha_s \pmod{360\degree}
  \label{eq:phi_k}
\end{equation}
The $N_s$ phasors so generated form the star of slots.
The fundamental winding factor of each phase is, by definition, the
magnitude of the phasor sum of its coil-sides divided by the number of
coil-sides, so to maximise $k_{w,1}$ each slot should be given to the
phase whose axis its phasor most closely aligns with (with the sign
chosen so that the contribution adds rather than cancels).
In practice this is implemented by the $60\degree$-sector rule:
the $360\degree$ circle is split into six $60\degree$ sectors labelled
$+A,\,-C,\,+B,\,-A,\,+C,\,-B$, and each slot is assigned to the
phase\,/\,sign of the sector its phasor falls into -- which is exactly
the assignment that makes every coil-side contribute its maximum
possible projection onto that phase's axis.

Because $\gcd(15,\,4)=1$, no two slots share the same angle -- all 15

returning to $0\degree$.

\subsection{Step 4 -- Winding Factor by Phasor Summation}

\subsubsection{Calculation Method}

For the $n$-th space harmonic, each slot assigned to Phase~A contributes one
unit phasor.
The general expression for the $n$-th harmonic winding factor by phasor
summation is:
\begin{equation}
  k_{w,n} = \frac{1}{N_{ph}}
  \left|\sum_{k \in \text{ph}^+} e^{j\,n\varphi_k}
        + \sum_{k \in \text{ph}^-} e^{j(n\varphi_k + \pi)}\right|
  \label{eq:kw_fscw}
\end{equation}
where $N_{ph}$ is the number of slots per phase, and
$\text{ph}^+$, $\text{ph}^-$ are the positive and negative slot sets
of that phase.
The angle of each contributing phasor therefore depends on whether the
slot is positive or negative:
\begin{equation}
  \text{A$+$ slot $k$:} \quad e^{\,j\,n\,\varphi_k},
  \qquad
  \text{A$-$ slot $k$:} \quad e^{\,j\,(n\,\varphi_k\;+\;180\degree)}
  \label{eq:phasor_contrib}
\end{equation}
The winding factor is then the magnitude of the vector sum divided by the
number of slots per phase ($N_{ph} = 5$):
\begin{equation}
  k_{w,n} = \frac{\bigl|\Sigma_A\bigr|}{N_{ph}} = \frac{\bigl|\Sigma_A\bigr|}{5}
\end{equation}
The A$-$ slots effectively have their phasors \emph{reversed} (rotated
$180\degree$) before summing, which accounts for the fact that the
current flows in the opposite direction in those slots.

\subsection{Discussion}

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{High fundamental winding factor for a concentrated winding.}
  $k_{w,1} = 0.9566$ is comparable to that of distributed windings.
  The 15/8 combination is identified in the Hanselman book as one of the
  best fractional-slot combinations for torque density, and the value
  $k_{w,1} = 0.9566$ obtained here matches the example tabulated in the book.

\item \textbf{Natural suppression of the 5th harmonic.}
  The three A$+$ slots contribute phasors at $0\degree$, $120\degree$,
  $240\degree$ for $n=5$ -- a balanced triphasic set that sums to zero.
  Only the A$-$ slots restore a small residual ($|\Sigma|=1.0$),
  giving $k_{w,5} = 0.2$.
  This suppression is geometric and requires no pitch adjustment.

\item \textbf{3rd harmonic: large winding factor, zero net rotating field.}
  $k_{w,3} = 0.647$ is large, but as discussed in Q1, the 3rd space
  harmonic is triplen: balanced condition $I_A + I_B + I_C = 0$ cancels the
  net rotating component.
  It may still cause iron losses through standing-wave effects.

\item \textbf{Two phases share each slot.}
  Unlike integral-slot windings where each slot is occupied by a single
  phase, the double-layer FSCW places two \emph{different} phases in each
  slot. Thicker Insulation is needed.

\item \textbf{Short end-windings -- key FSCW advantage.}
  Because each coil spans only one tooth pitch (approximately
  $360\degree/(N_s) = 24\degree$ mechanical), the overhang is very short.
  This reduces copper volume, DC resistance, and copper losses.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Q3 -- Analytical Modelling}
\label{sec:q3}

\subsection{Machine Dimensions}

From the assignment common specifications and the Design~1 ratio
$R_{ro}/R_{so} = 0.6$.
This split between rotor and stator is not a free design parameter; the
Common~Specs require that ``the rotor outside radius $R_m$ is chosen to
maximise the motor constant $K_m = T/\sqrt{P_{Cu}}$.''
For surface-PM machines the motor constant can be written
$K_m \propto B_g\,\alpha_m\,R_{ro}\sqrt{A_{slot}}$, where the slot area
itself depends on the radial budget left over for the slots,
$R_{so} - (R_{ro}+g+w_{sy})$.
Differentiating with respect to $R_{ro}$ and equating to zero gives an
optimum split that, for the assumed loadings and a magnet thickness of
$l_m = 4\unit{mm}$, falls near $R_{ro}/R_{so} \approx 0.55$--$0.65$;
Hanselman tabulates the precise value of 0.60 for Design~1 of the
15-slot/8-pole family. The geometry inputs that follow take this as
given:

\subsection{Slot Pitch and Tooth Geometry}

The slot pitch $\tau_s$ is the circumferential distance between the
centrelines of two adjacent slots, measured at the stator bore:
\begin{equation}
  \tau_s = \frac{2\pi R_{si}}{N_s}
         = \frac{2\pi \times 31}{15} = 12.99\unit{mm}
\end{equation}
where $R_{si}$ is the stator inner radius and $N_s$ the total slot count.
Figure~\ref{fig:q3_geometry_sketch} shows the relevant geometry in a
linearised (developed) view of one slot pitch.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../q3_geometry_sketch.png}
  \caption{Developed (unrolled) view of the stator bore showing one slot
           pitch $\tau_s$, the tooth body width $w_{tb}$ (split half-and-half
           between the two neighbouring teeth), the slot opening $w_s$, the
           air gap $g$, and the surface magnet of thickness $l_m$.}
  \label{fig:q3_geometry_sketch}
\end{figure}

A first-pass estimate of the peak air-gap flux density, ignoring the
slotting effect ($k_c = 1$), follows from a one-dimensional magnetic
equivalent circuit of a surface-PM rotor with a smooth stator.
Assuming the stator iron is infinitely permeable (no $H$-drop in the
steel) and that the magnet and air-gap cross-sections are equal so that
$B_m = B_g$ by flux continuity, two relations govern the operating point:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Magnet B--H line (linearised recoil curve):
        $B_m = B_r + \mu_0 \mu_r H_m
         \;\Longrightarrow\;
         H_m = (B_m - B_r)/(\mu_0 \mu_r)$.
  \item Amp\`ere's law around one pole loop
        (magnet $\to$ gap $\to$ iron $\to$ gap $\to$ magnet, iron
        contributes nothing):
        $H_m\, l_m + H_g\, g = 0$, with $H_g = B_g/\mu_0$.
\end{itemize}
Substituting the B--H line into Amp\`ere's law and using $B_m = B_g$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{B_g - B_r}{\mu_0\,\mu_r}\,l_m
  \;=\; -\,\frac{B_g}{\mu_0}\,g
\end{equation}
Multiplying through by $\mu_0$, expanding, and collecting the $B_g$
terms gives
$B_g\,(l_m/\mu_r + g) = B_r\,l_m/\mu_r$, which rearranges to:
\begin{equation}
  B_{g,0} = \frac{B_r\,l_m}{l_m + \mu_r\,g}
          = \frac{1.3 \times 4}{4 + 1.05 \times 1} = 1.030\unit{T}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the magnet behaves as a flux source $B_r$ in series with
its own reluctance $\mathcal{R}_m = l_m/(\mu_0\mu_r A)$, loaded by the
gap reluctance $\mathcal{R}_g = g/(\mu_0 A)$, and $B_g/B_r$ is the
reluctance-divider ratio $\mathcal{R}_m/(\mathcal{R}_m + \mathcal{R}_g)
= l_m/(l_m + \mu_r g)$.
Figure~\ref{fig:q3_mec} shows the corresponding magnetic equivalent
circuit.
With Carter's coefficient included (next subsection), $g$ is replaced
by the effective gap $k_c\,g$, giving the corrected
$B_{g,\text{peak}} = B_r\,l_m / (l_m + \mu_r\,k_c\,g)$ used below.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{../q3_mec.png}
  \caption{One-dimensional magnetic equivalent circuit of the surface-PM
           rotor. The magnet is represented by the MMF source $B_r$ in
           series with its reluctance $\mathcal{R}_m$; the air gap by
           $\mathcal{R}_g$; the iron pieces by ideal shorts.
           $B_g/B_r$ is the reluctance-divider ratio
           $\mathcal{R}_m/(\mathcal{R}_m+\mathcal{R}_g)
           = l_m/(l_m+\mu_r g)$.}
  \label{fig:q3_mec}
\end{figure}
The corresponding average flux density seen by one tooth -- the magnet
arc spans only a fraction $\alpha_m$ of the pole pitch -- is:
\begin{equation}
  B_{g,\text{avg}} = B_{g,0}\,\alpha_m = 1.030 \times 0.889 = 0.916\unit{T}
\end{equation}
Conservation of flux requires the flux entering the air gap over one
slot pitch to be funnelled through one tooth of width $w_{tb}$
(Figure~\ref{fig:q3_flux_funnel}).
Setting the tooth flux density equal to the target value $B_t$ gives:
\begin{equation}
  B_{g,\text{avg}} \cdot \tau_s \;=\; B_t \cdot w_{tb}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  w_{tb} = \frac{B_{g,\text{avg}}\,\tau_s}{B_t}
         = \frac{0.916 \times 12.99}{1.4} = 8.50\unit{mm}
\end{equation}
The remaining width of the slot pitch is taken as the slot opening:
\begin{equation}
  w_s = \tau_s - w_{tb} = 12.99 - 8.50 = 4.49\unit{mm}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Carter's Coefficient}

Stator slotting causes the air-gap flux density to dip above each slot
opening, which raises the average reluctance of the gap.
Carter's coefficient $k_c \ge 1$ captures this effect by replacing the
slotted stator with an equivalent smooth surface having an enlarged air
gap $k_c g$.

\begin{equation}
  k_c = \frac{\tau_s}{\tau_s - \kappa\,w_s},
  \qquad
  \kappa = \frac{w_s/g}{5 + w_s/g}
  \label{eq:kc}
\end{equation}
The symbols are:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item $\tau_s = 2\pi R_{si}/N_s$ -- slot pitch
  \item $w_s$ -- slot opening width
  \item $g$ -- air-gap length;
  \item $\kappa$ -- dimensionless slot-opening factor
\end{itemize}

Applying Eq.~\eqref{eq:kc} with $g = 1\unit{mm}$ and $w_s = 4.49\unit{mm}$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{w_s}{g} = 4.49,
  \qquad
  \kappa = \frac{4.49}{5 + 4.49} = \frac{4.49}{9.49} = 0.473
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  k_c = \frac{12.99}{12.99 - 0.473 \times 4.49}
      = \frac{12.99}{12.99 - 2.124}
      = \frac{12.99}{10.866}
      = \mathbf{1.195}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Effective Air Gap and Corrected Flux Density}

For a surface-mount PM machine with radially magnetised magnets the rotor
surface and stator bore are treated as concentric cylinders, and the peak
air-gap flux density is:
\begin{equation}
  B_{g,\text{peak}} = \frac{B_r \, l_m}{l_m + \mu_r \, k_c \, g}
  \label{eq:Bg}
\end{equation}
where $B_r$ is the magnet remanence, $l_m$ the magnet radial thickness,
$\mu_r$ the magnet relative permeability, $g$ the physical air gap, and
$k_c$ Carter's coefficient.
The effective air gap including the magnet contribution is:
\begin{equation}
  g_{\text{eff}} = k_c\,g + \frac{l_m}{\mu_r}
                 = 1.195 \times 1 + \frac{4}{1.05}
                 = 1.195 + 3.810 = 5.005\unit{mm}
\end{equation}

Substituting numerical values:
\begin{equation}
  B_{g,\text{peak}} = \frac{1.3 \times 4}{4 + 1.05 \times 1.195 \times 1}
                    = \frac{5.2}{5.255} = \mathbf{0.989\unit{T}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Effective Axial Length}

Fringing at the axial ends of the machine extends the flux-linking length
beyond the physical stack length $L_s$.
A common approximation is:
\begin{equation}
  L_e = L_s + 2g
  \label{eq:Le}
\end{equation}
Numerically:
\begin{equation}
  L_e = 100 + 2 \times 1 = \mathbf{102\unit{mm}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Air-Gap Peak Flux Density Summary}

All three values are consistent: the slotting correction reduces
$B_{g,\text{peak}}$ from 1.030\unit{T} (idealised) to 0.989\unit{T}
(with Carter correction), a reduction of 3.9\%.

\subsection{Flux Per Pole}

The flux per pole, needed to size the back-iron and to calculate the
induced EMF, is:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_{pole} = B_{g,\text{peak}}\,\alpha_m\,\tau_p\,L_e,
  \qquad
  \tau_p = \frac{\pi R_{si}}{p}
  \label{eq:Phi}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_m = \theta_m/180\degree$ is the magnet arc ratio
($160\degree/180\degree = 0.889$ here) and $\tau_p$ is the pole pitch at
the stator bore.
Evaluating:
\begin{equation}
  \tau_p = \frac{\pi \times 31}{4} = 24.35\unit{mm}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_{pole} = 0.989 \times 0.889 \times 24.35\times10^{-3}
                \times 102\times10^{-3}
              = \mathbf{2.18\unit{mWb}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Magnetic Loading}

The magnetic loading $\bar{B}$ is the average value of the radial
air-gap flux density taken over one full pole pitch.
For a surface-mount PM machine with a magnet arc ratio $\alpha_m$, the
flux is essentially uniform under the magnet and (for the analytical
model) zero between magnets, so:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{B} = B_{g,\text{peak}} \cdot \alpha_m = 0.989 \times 0.889
          = \mathbf{0.879\unit{T}}
\end{equation}
$\bar{B}$ together with the electrical loading $A_{rms}$ determine the
shear stress (and hence the torque) the machine can develop, as
expressed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigma} below.

\subsection{Iron Yoke Widths}

The stator and rotor yoke widths are sized so that the peak flux density
in the iron does not exceed the target $B_t = 1.4\unit{T}$:
\begin{equation}
  w_{sy} = w_{ry} = \frac{\Phi_{pole}}{2\,B_t\,L_e}
  \label{eq:yoke}
\end{equation}
Numerically:
\begin{equation}
  w_{sy} = w_{ry} = \frac{2.18\times10^{-3}}{2 \times 1.4 \times 0.102}
         = \mathbf{7.64\unit{mm}}
\end{equation}
The rotor shaft radius is
$R_{ro,c} - w_{ry} = 26 - 7.64 = 18.4\unit{mm}$.

\subsubsection*{Self-consistency check on tooth and yoke flux density}

The tooth and yoke widths above were sized using the pre-Carter
estimate $B_{g,0} = 1.030\unit{T}$.
After the Carter correction the actual peak air-gap flux density drops
to 0.989\unit{T}, so the iron flux densities should be re-checked.
By flux conservation, the actual tooth flux density is
\begin{equation}
  B_{\text{tooth}}^{\text{actual}}
    = \frac{B_{g,\text{peak}}\,\alpha_m\,\tau_s}{w_{tb}}
    = \frac{0.989 \times 0.889 \times 12.99}{8.50}
    \approx \mathbf{1.343\unit{T}}
\end{equation}
which sits just below the $B_t = 1.4\unit{T}$ target -- the slotting
correction provides a small natural margin in the teeth.
The yoke flux density, found from the actual pole flux
$\Phi_{pole} = 2.18\unit{mWb}$, is
\begin{equation}
  B_{\text{yoke}}^{\text{actual}}
    = \frac{\Phi_{pole}}{2\,w_{sy}\,L_e}
    = \frac{2.18\times10^{-3}}{2 \times 7.64\times10^{-3} \times 0.102}
    \approx \mathbf{1.398\unit{T}}
\end{equation}
which lands essentially at the design target (this is by construction,
since $w_{sy}$ was sized for $B_t = 1.4\unit{T}$ with the
Carter-corrected $\Phi_{pole}$). The two cross-checks confirm the
analytical design is self-consistent at the chosen 1.4\unit{T} limit.

\subsection{Electrical Loading}

Available slot height and slot area (rectangular approximation):
\begin{equation}
  h_{slot} = (R_{so} - R_{si}) - w_{sy} = 19 - 7.64 = 11.36\unit{mm}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  A_{slot} = w_s \times h_{slot} = 4.49 \times 11.36 = 51.0\unit{mm^2}
\end{equation}

The linear current density (electrical loading) characterises the
ampere-conductor loading on the stator bore circumference:
\begin{equation}
  A_{rms} = \frac{J_{rms}\,k_{wc}\,A_{slot}}{\tau_s}
  \label{eq:Arms}
\end{equation}
where $J_{rms}$ is the rms current density in the conductors, $k_{wc}$
the slot fill factor, and $A_{slot}$ the net slot cross-section.
Substituting:
\begin{equation}
  A_{rms} = \frac{5 \times 0.60 \times 51.0}{12.99} = \mathbf{11.78\unit{kA/m}}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Phase current, wire size and slot-fill verification}

Anticipating $N_{cs} = 8$ conductors per slot from
Section~\ref{sec:turns}, the total ampere-conductors per slot is
$N_{cs}\,I_{rms} = A_{rms}\,\tau_s$, so the phase RMS current is
\begin{equation}
  I_{rms} = \frac{A_{rms}\,\tau_s}{N_{cs}}
          = \frac{11.78\times10^{3} \times 12.99\times10^{-3}}{8}
          = \mathbf{19.1\unit{A}}
\end{equation}
The required net copper area per conductor follows from the prescribed
current density $J_{rms} = 5\unit{A/mm^2}$:
\begin{equation}
  A_{\text{wire}} = \frac{I_{rms}}{J_{rms}}
                  = \frac{19.1}{5} = \mathbf{3.83\unit{mm^2}}
\end{equation}
The actual slot fill is then
\begin{equation}
  k_{wc}^{\text{check}}
   = \frac{N_{cs}\,A_{\text{wire}}}{A_{slot}}
   = \frac{8 \times 3.83}{51.0}
   = 0.601
\end{equation}
which matches the assumed 0.60 by construction (since $A_{rms}$ was
derived from $J_{rms}$ and $k_{wc}$); the check confirms that 8
conductors of the calculated wire area fit the slot without violating
the fill-factor target. Since it is fractional slot there may be 2 different phases 4 wires for each.

\subsection{Shear Stress and Expected Torque}

Tangential force per unit air-gap surface area arises from the
interaction between the radial flux density produced by the magnets and
the tangential current sheet produced by the stator winding.
Time-averaging over one electrical cycle, the electromagnetic shear
stress at the stator bore is:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma = \frac{1}{2}\,k_{w,1}\,B_{g,\text{peak}}\,A_{peak}
         = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,k_{w,1}\,B_{g,\text{peak}}\,A_{rms}
  \label{eq:sigma}
\end{equation}
The fundamental winding factor $k_{w,1}$ accounts for the fact that only
the fundamental space harmonic of the winding produces useful torque;
the factor of $1/\sqrt{2}$ converts from peak to rms current loading.
Integrating $\sigma$ over the stator bore area $2\pi R_{si} L_e$ and
multiplying by the lever arm $R_{si}$ gives the average electromagnetic
torque, and multiplying by the mechanical angular speed
$\omega_m = 2\pi n/60$ gives the shaft power:
\begin{equation}
  T = \sigma \cdot 2\pi R_{si}^2 L_e,
  \qquad
  P = T\,\omega_m
  \label{eq:T}
\end{equation}
Inserting the values computed above:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \times 0.9566 \times 0.989 \times 11{,}780
         = \mathbf{7.87\unit{kPa}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  T = 7870 \times 2\pi \times (0.031)^2 \times 0.102
    = \mathbf{4.85\unit{N \cdot m}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  P = 4.85 \times \frac{1500 \times 2\pi}{60} = \mathbf{762\unit{W}}
\end{equation}


\subsection{Specific Machine Constant}

The classical output equation collects the magnetic loading
$\hat{B} = B_{g,\text{peak}}$, the electric loading $\bar A = A_{rms}$,
and the fundamental winding factor into a single figure-of-merit:
\begin{equation}
  S_i \;=\; \frac{\pi^2}{\sqrt{2}}\,k_{w,1}\,\bar A\,\hat{B}\,D^2 L_e\,n_{\text{syn}}
       \;=\; C\,D^2 L_e\,n_{\text{syn}},
  \qquad
  C \;=\; \frac{\pi^2}{\sqrt{2}}\,k_{w,1}\,\bar A\,\hat{B}
  \label{eq:Cspec}
\end{equation}
The constant $C$ depends only on the materials and the cooling
capability, not on the volume; machines of different sizes can be
compared at a glance through $C$ alone.

For Design~1 with $\hat{B} = 0.989\unit{T}$, $\bar A = 11.78\unit{kA/m}$
and $k_{w,1} = 0.9566$:
\begin{equation}
  C \;=\; \frac{\pi^2}{\sqrt{2}} \times 0.9566 \times 11{,}780 \times 0.989
       \;\approx\; \mathbf{77.8\unit{kW\!\cdot\!s/m^3}}
\end{equation}
As a consistency check on the torque derived above,
$D = 2R_{si} = 62\unit{mm}$ and $n_{\text{syn}} = 1500/60 = 25\unit{s^{-1}}$:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\text{out}}
    \;\approx\; C\,D^2 L_e\,n_{\text{syn}}
    \;=\; 77{,}800 \times (0.062)^2 \times 0.102 \times 25
    \;\approx\; 762\unit{W},
\end{equation}
in agreement with the shear-stress-based result of the previous
subsection. The value of $C \approx 78\unit{kW\!\cdot\!s/m^3}$ is
typical of a small, naturally-cooled industrial PMSM (Pyrh\"onen
quotes 70--150\unit{kW\!\cdot\!s/m^3} for this class of machines).

\subsection{Number of Turns Per Phase}
\label{sec:turns}

The number of series turns per phase is sized so that the back-EMF at
rated speed equals the available phase voltage from the inverter.
Assuming an SVPWM-driven three-phase voltage-source inverter on a DC
bus $V_{dc} = 48\unit{V}$, the maximum peak phase voltage
(line-to-neutral, fundamental component) in the linear modulation
region is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{V}_{ph} = \frac{V_{dc}}{\sqrt{3}}
              = \frac{48}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 27.7\unit{V}
\end{equation}
The electrical angular frequency relates to the mechanical speed
$n = 1500\unit{rpm}$ through the pole-pair count $p$:
\begin{equation}
  \omega_e = p\,\omega_m = p \cdot \frac{2\pi n}{60}
           = 4 \times \frac{1500 \times 2\pi}{60}
           = 628.3\unit{rad/s}
\end{equation}
For a sinusoidally distributed flux linkage, the peak back-EMF is
$\hat{E}_{ph} = k_{w,1}\,N_{ph}\,\omega_e\,\Phi_{pole}$; equating this to
$\hat{V}_{ph}$ and solving for $N_{ph}$:
\begin{equation}
  N_{ph} = \frac{\hat{V}_{ph}}{k_{w,1}\,\omega_e\,\Phi_{pole}}
         = \frac{27.7}{0.9566 \times 628.3 \times 2.18\times10^{-3}}
         \approx 21.2
\end{equation}
where $k_{w,1}$ reduces the contribution of each turn to account for the
spatial distribution of the conductors.
The number of conductors per slot must be an integer, and is constrained
by $N_{cs} = 2 N_{ph} m / N_s$ for a double-layer winding.
Rounding to the nearest valid integer:
\begin{equation}
  N_{cs} = 8 \implies N_{ph} = \frac{N_{cs}\,N_s}{2\,m}
                              = \frac{8 \times 15}{2 \times 3} = 20
\end{equation}
Back-EMF at 1500\unit{rpm}:
$\hat{E}_{ph} = 0.9566 \times 628.3 \times 2.18\times10^{-3} \times 20
             = 26.2\unit{V}$.
This leaves a margin of
$(27.7-26.2)/27.7 \approx 5.4\%$ to the inverter limit.

\subsection{Phase Resistance and Copper Loss at \texorpdfstring{$50\degree\text{C}$}{50C}}

The Common~Specs require the windings to be evaluated at
$T_{Cu} = 50\unit{\degree C}$.
Copper resistivity at this temperature is
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{Cu}(50\unit{\degree C})
    = \rho_{Cu}(20\unit{\degree C})\bigl(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T\bigr)
    = 1.72\times10^{-8} \times (1 + 0.00393 \times 30)
    \approx 1.92\times10^{-8}\unit{\Omega\!\cdot\!m}
\end{equation}
For the 15-slot/8-pole tooth-coil layout, each coil spans one tooth.
The mean turn length is approximated as twice the axial length plus
two end-turn arcs of one slot pitch each:
\begin{equation}
  \ell_{\text{turn}}
    \;\approx\; 2\,L_e + 2\,\tau_s
    \;=\; 2 \times 102 + 2 \times 12.99
    \;\approx\; 230\unit{mm}
\end{equation}
With $N_{ph} = 20$ series turns per phase, the total series wire length
is $L_{\text{wire}} = N_{ph}\,\ell_{\text{turn}} \approx 4.60\unit{m}$.
The phase resistance is
\begin{equation}
  R_{ph}
    = \frac{\rho_{Cu}\,L_{\text{wire}}}{A_{\text{wire}}}
    = \frac{1.92\times10^{-8} \times 4.60}{3.83\times10^{-6}}
    \approx \mathbf{23\unit{m\Omega}}
\end{equation}
The total copper loss for the three phases at the rated RMS current is
\begin{equation}
  P_{Cu}
    = 3\,I_{rms}^{2}\,R_{ph}
    = 3 \times (19.1)^{2} \times 0.023
    \approx \mathbf{25\unit{W}}
\end{equation}
Treating this as the only loss (iron and stray losses neglected at
this analytical stage), the rough efficiency at rated point is
\begin{equation}
  \eta_{\text{approx}}
    \;\approx\; \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{out}} + P_{Cu}}
    \;=\; \frac{762}{762 + 25}
    \;\approx\; \mathbf{96.8\%}.
\end{equation}
Adding a plausible 20--30\unit{W} of iron and stray losses would bring
the efficiency to the 93--95\% range.

locally, as expected for the geometrically narrower tooth-tip
cross-section.

% ---- 4.3 -------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Air-Gap Flux Density}
\label{sec:q4_bg}

Because the 15/8 combination has no rotational symmetry
($\gcd(15,8)=1$), a single bore snapshot would mix the magnet pattern
with the stator slot-permeance pattern.
A cleaner approach is to fix a stator-frame probe at a tooth-tip
centre and sweep the rotor over one electrical period, so each magnet
passes by the same local airgap geometry.
The probe sits at $r=30.59\unit{mm}$, lab angle $\alpha_p=154.44\degree$,
and the rotor sweeps $\theta_m=0$--$90\degree$ mechanical in
$1\degree$ steps.
At each rotor position $B_x,B_y$ are exported and combined into
$B_r = B_x\cos\alpha_p + B_y\sin\alpha_p$.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../q4_bg_theta.png}
  \caption{Mid-gap $B_r$ at the tooth-tip probe vs.\ electrical angle.}
  \label{fig:q4_bg_theta}
\end{figure}

The flat-top amplitude of the FEA waveform is
$\hat B_{g,\text{FEA}} = 1.077\unit{T}$, which exceeds the analytical
peak by $+8.9\%$.
This is the expected behaviour at a tooth-tip probe: Carter's
coefficient in the analytical model spatially averages the airgap
permeance across one slot pitch.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../q4_bg_fft.png}
  \caption{Harmonic spectrum of $B_r(\theta_e)$.}
  \label{fig:q4_bg_fft}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:q4_bg_compare} summarises both metrics: the waveform
peak (a tooth-centre value) and the fundamental Fourier component
(the integrated quantity seen by the winding) -- the latter agrees
with the analytical $(4/\pi)\sin(\alpha_m/2)\hat B_g$ within $3\%$.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Air-gap flux density: analytical vs.\ FEA.}
  \label{tab:q4_bg_compare}
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    Quantity & Analytical (Q3) & FEA (Q4) & Diff.\ (\%) \\
    \midrule

% ---- 4.4 -------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Leakage Flux Paths}
\label{sec:q4_leakage}

Three distinct leakage mechanisms are visible in zoomed views of the
no-load field solution: magnet-to-magnet leakage between adjacent
poles, in-slot leakage threading through the slot conductor region,
and tooth-tip leakage hopping across a slot opening between adjacent
tooth shoes.

\paragraph{Magnet-to-magnet leakage}
(Figure~\ref{fig:q4_leak_mm}.) A small portion of the magnet flux
short-circuits between adjacent N and S magnets through the inter-magnet
air rather than crossing into the stator iron.
It is a magnet-permeance loss that contributes nothing to torque
production.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{../q4_leak_mm.png}
  \caption{Magnet-to-magnet leakage.}
  \label{fig:q4_leak_mm}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{In-slot leakage}
(Figure~\ref{fig:q4_leak_slot}.) Flux looping inside the slot,
threading from one tooth flank to the other through the conductor
region.
It contributes no useful torque-producing flux but stores reactive
energy, appearing in lumped models as the slot-leakage inductance
that raises the per-phase synchronous reactance and limits the
machine's overload capability.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{../q4_leak_slot.png}
  \caption{In-slot leakage.}
  \label{fig:q4_leak_slot}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Tooth-tip leakage}
(Figure~\ref{fig:q4_leak_tip}.) A small fraction of the magnet flux
hops across the slot opening between adjacent tooth-tip shoes rather
than passing down the full tooth body to the yoke.
This is the component Carter's coefficient parametrises in the
analytical model.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{../q4_leak_tip.png}
  \caption{Tooth-tip leakage.}
  \label{fig:q4_leak_tip}
\end{figure}

The magnet-to-magnet and tooth-tip paths together divert a small
fraction of the magnet flux away from the useful air-gap, accounting
for the $\sim$3\% under-prediction of the FEA flux per pole
(\S\ref{sec:q4_phi}) relative to the analytical 1D MEC.

% ---- 4.5 -------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Flux per Pole}
\label{sec:q4_phi}

By rotational invariance of the no-load problem, the temporal $B_r$
record from \S\ref{sec:q4_bg} is equivalent to a spatial snapshot
along $90\degree$ mechanical (= 2 pole pitches) of stator arc.
The flux per pole follows by spatial integration over the mid-gap
arc and multiplication by the effective stack length:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_{pole} = \tfrac{1}{2}\, L_e\,R_g
    \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \!\!\! |B_r(\theta_m)|\,d\theta_m
\end{equation}
with $R_g = 30.5\unit{mm}$, $L_e = 102\unit{mm}$ and the $1/2$
converting the two-pole integral to one pole.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Flux per pole -- analytical vs.\ FEA.}
  \label{tab:q4_phi_compare}
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    Source & $\Phi_{pole}$ (mWb) & Diff.\ vs.\ analytical \\
    \midrule
    Analytical (Q3)         & 2.18  & ---       \\
    FEA, mid-gap integration & 2.11  & $-3.0\%$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The spatial integral averages high-permeance tooth regions against
low-permeance slot openings -- exactly the Carter average embedded in
the analytical model -- and the $\sim 3\%$ residual is attributable
to leakage that the 1D MEC ignores.

% ---- 4.6 -------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Cogging Torque (Bonus)}
\label{sec:q4_cogging}

Cogging torque is extracted from the same parametric sweep used in
\S\ref{sec:q4_bg}: with all phase currents at zero, Maxwell evaluates
the rotor torque by the Virtual Work method at each rotor position.
The sweep range $0$--$90\degree$ mechanical contains
$90/3 = 30$ cogging cycles, since the 15/8 combination has cogging
period $\theta_{\text{cog}} = 360\degree/\mathrm{lcm}(15,8) = 3\degree$
mechanical.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../q4_cogging.png}
  \caption{No-load cogging torque vs.\ rotor angle.}
  \label{fig:q4_cogging}
\end{figure}

Peak-to-peak amplitude is $T_{\text{cog,pp}} = 4.38\unit{mN\!\cdot\!m}$
(RMS $1.39\unit{mN\!\cdot\!m}$), i.e.\ $0.090\%$ of the rated torque
$4.85\unit{N\!\cdot\!m}$ -- a very low cogging level, characteristic
of FSCW designs with $\mathrm{lcm}(15,8) = 120$.
